Evenly waitlist page — three directions

All three sell the same two things in the same order: snap a receipt and it lists the items, then everyone else claims what they ate in a browser, no app, no account. What differs is how hard the page leans on the demo, the objection, or the atmosphere. Palette and components are lifted from design/web-claim-mockup.html, so whichever wins already matches the product.

Shared rules applied to all three, from what actually converts on pre-launch pages: one job per page and no nav links to leak clicks, the email field above the fold, a headline that names the outcome instead of the category, one field only (email), social proof near the button, and a success state that immediately asks for a share. Typical waitlist pages convert around 2%; the ones that clear 20% do these five things.

Direction A

Show the trick — "Snap it. They claim it."

The hero is the feature: a receipt with a scan beam running down it, and the itemised list assembling underneath while you read the headline. Nobody has to imagine what the app does. Best choice if most traffic is cold (Reddit, TikTok, Product Hunt) and you get one scroll-free second to land the idea. Risk: demo-led heroes live or die on the demo feeling real, so the item names have to be real receipt junk, not "Item 1".

Direction B

Kill the objection — "Only one of you needs the app"

Leads with the reason group-expense apps die: you cannot get five people to install anything. The hero is a diagram, one phone with the app fanning out to four plain browser links, each showing who has already claimed. Best if the audience has already tried Splitwise and given up, and if you plan to run comparison ads. Risk: it names a problem before it names the product, so the receipt-scan feature has to arrive immediately after or the page sells the wrong hero feature.

Direction C

Be the object — the page is a receipt

The whole page is a thermal receipt on a dark table: torn edges, monospace, dashed rules. The feature list is printed as line items with claim tags, the signup is where the total goes, and the barcode is the waitlist number. Most memorable and most shareable, which is what actually moves a pre-launch page; also the most opinionated. Risk: a novelty layout can read as a gimmick and it constrains how much copy fits, so it only works while the pitch stays this short. Committed single-theme by design — the receipt is white on a dark table in both light and dark mode.

After the email

The success state is the growth loop

Whichever direction wins, the page after submit should not say "thanks". It should show your position in the queue and offer a share link that moves you up — that one block is the difference between a list that grows from ads and one that grows from itself. In C it is already drawn: the barcode becomes your guest number.
